Project Writeup

Design Rationale

After many drafts and rounds of exploratory graphs and interactive visualizations, we decided to use a line chart as our primary visualization. It presents temporal trends in precipitation very clearly and makes it easy to compare regions and scenarios. At first, we tried a world map visualization to show overall changes in preciptation, but the results were not very informative because precipitation changes were subtle, and often saturated. We then shifted to an area plot that showed every individual city, but the level of detail made the visualization hard to read as there were thousands of cities that didn’t necessarily mean anything to the user. This led us to group cities into broader regions as it would provide more context and make more sense to the users.

The continuous lines help users follow changes and compare historical patterns with future projections. We encoded scenarios with distinct colors: gray for historical data, blue for low emission (SSP 126), and red for high emission (SSP 585). These choices align with intuitive associations where warmer colors suggest stronger impact. A vertical dashed line at 2014 marks the boundary between historical observations and future projections. We also added optional regression curves to help users see long term trends when the data is highly variable.

To support meaningful exploration, we added several interactive features that help users control what they see. The year window lets viewers adjust the temporal range, which is helpful because precipitation can look very different when comparing short segments with long term trends. Tooltips provide detailed values on hover without cluttering the chart. We split the data into four regions of the United State because regional patterns reflect climate change signals more reliably than city level data, which is often influenced by local noise. To help viewers move through these regions, we built a carousel-style navigator with buttons and dots that encourages exploration and comparison among regions. The legend toggle allows users to show or hide scenarios so they can isolate differences between historical observations, low emission futures, and high emission futures. Our color palette follows conventions in climate modeling, with red for high emission, blue for low emission, and black or gray for historical data. These choices support clarity, interpretability, and a good user experience throughout the visualization.

Development Process Overview

Our development process involved multiple iterations experimenting with different graphs and data manipulations. We began with exploratory data analysis (EDA) using tools such as pandas and matplotlib to draft visualizations to understand the dataset structure. From there, we built early interactive visualizations, tested various visual encodings, and refined the UI/UX to create the final visualization. Work was distributed with Claudine cleaning and standardizing datasets and separating emission scenarios, Sharon handling data processing and initial D3 structure, Charles implementing interactions and regression features, and Jeremy focusing on UI polish and refining graphs. EDA was the most time-consuming part, taking approximately eight people-hours as we navigated through the large, multi-file dataset. For the checkpoint, we used map-based visualizations, but found they required excessive interactivity and zooming which hurt UX. Similarly, our initial NameGrapher-style approach had too many data points, leading us to develop our regional grouping approach. After reiving feedback from the checkpoint and from the professor, we quickly converged on the idea for our final visualization. The conceptual design took only about fifteen minutes to sketch out, but implementing it required significantly more effort. Building the final version, integrating all interactions, and ensuring UI consistency took our team roughly four hours of work. Lastly, adding the finishing touches on the UI and adding features to improve UX took another hour. So, it roughly took us around fourteen to fifteen hours to complete.
