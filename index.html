Project Writeup

Design Rationale

After numerous drafts and exploratory graphs and interactive visualizations, we decided to conclude with using a line chart as our primary visual encoding because it effectively displays temporal trends in precipitation data across multiple regions and scenarios. At first, we experimented with a NameGrapher-like graph, plotting each individual cities. However, the granularity of this visualization was far too granular, leading us to scope out and group cities into regions. The continuous lines allow users to easily track changes over time and compare historical patterns with future projections. We encoded different scenarios using distinct colors: gray for historical data, red for low-emission scenarios (SSP 126), and blue for high-emission scenarios (SSP 585), following intuitive color associations where warmer colors suggest higher impact (precipitation levels). A dashed vertical line at 2014 separates historical data from future projections, as the historical data cut off at 2014. Furthermore, we added optional regression curves (dashed lines) to help users identify overall trends. This is especially useful when the data points contains high variability. For interactions, we implemented clickable legend items to toggle scenarios independently, allowing users to focus on specific comparisons between scenarios. The region selector with navigation buttons and dots enables easy exploration across the four US regions. We also included a year range filter to let users zoom into specific time periods of interest. To ensure visual continuity, we connected the future projection lines to the last historical data point, creating seamless transitions at the 2014 boundary. We also converted precipitation values from scientific notation to mm/day units for better readability, making the y-axis values more intuitive for readers. The legend items are initialized as selected on page load to accurately reflect the displayed data, and tooltips on hover provide detailed precipitation values for data exploration. It presents temporal trends in precipitation very clearly and makes it easy to compare regions and scenarios. At first, we tried a NameGrapher-style plot that showed every individual city, but the level of detail made the visualization hard to read as there were thousands of cities that didn’t necessarily mean anything to the user. This led us to group cities into broader regions as it would provide more context and make more sense to the users. To support meaningful exploration, we added several interactive features that help users control what they see. The year window lets viewers adjust the temporal range, which is helpful because precipitation can look very different when comparing short segments with long term trends. Tooltips provide detailed values on hover without cluttering the chart. We split the data into four regions of the United State because regional patterns reflect climate change signals more reliably than city level data, which is often influenced by local noise. To help viewers move through these regions, we built a carousel-style navigator with buttons and dots that encourages exploration and comparison among regions. The legend toggle allows users to show or hide scenarios so they can isolate differences between historical observations, low emission futures, and high emission futures. Our color palette follows conventions in climate modeling, with red for high emission, blue for low emission, and black or gray for historical data. These choices support clarity, interpretability, and a good user experience throughout the visualization.

Development Process Overview

Our development process took place through multiple iterations and renditions of experimenting with different graphs and data manipulations. We began with exploratory data analysis (EDA) and a series of draft visualizations to understand the structure and content of the dataset. From there, we built early interactive visualizations, tested various visual encodings, and ultimately refined the UI and UX to create the final visualization. Performing EDA was the most time consuming and challenging part of the project. Because this dataset was so large and contained so many files and resources on just to navigate through it, we spent around one whole day dedicated to understanding what we were working with.
